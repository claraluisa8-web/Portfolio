Design Library · Research · 2025

Students mostly work alone, but say they want to work together.

Before the Design Library in Brooks Hall is renovated, the Design Council asked our Research Methods class how its main study lounge would need to change to stay useful to the students, faculty, and staff who rely on it. Our team focused on seating, investigating how it could better serve undergraduate students who come to the library to work in groups. We worked the question through observation, synthesis, and the creation of a method of our own. Three teams shared one survey, each with a different slice.

What we were asked

For the undergraduates it serves in the College of Design, the lounge is a place to study and to work together, and the renovation is a chance to reshape it. We framed the problem this way: undergraduate students need more flexible and varied seating, because it would help them find a space for planned group work and thus foster connection and the exchange of knowledge.

To understand why the problem mattered, we asked “Five Whys,” and each answer pointed the same way:

  1. 1Students differ in their needs from a study space.
  2. 2Their seating needs depend on whether they are alone or with others.
  3. 3Collaborative tasks necessitate open spaces for easy interaction.
  4. 4Convenient spatial access helps them build social connections and a sense of belonging.
  5. 5Feeling connected and comfortable makes students more likely to share ideas and learn from one another.

team vs. independent work

Our team (Willow Ahrens, Tushita Kaul, Diya Franklin, and I) completed a total of nine fly-on-the-wall sessions, three of them mine, on September 10, October 1, and October 6, 2025. I also coded survey responses for the content analysis, where my initials (CM) mark each row. Although we conducted all of the research methods as a team, we created independent visualizations and gave different recommendations in our reports.

limitations

Several limits shaped what this study can claim. Most of the primary data, specifically the survey responses and pop-up interview transcripts, was provided to, rather than collected by, us, so we had no say over question design or how complete the answers were, and much of what we analyzed came without demographic detail. We drafted structured, semi-structured, and unstructured questions instead to show where future research could go. Our observations also covered only September and the first week of October, a short snapshot that can miss the swings that come with class schedules and deadlines.

What we found

Most students use the library to study alone and in silence, even though it is meant to be a shared academic environment, and that quiet atmosphere was frequently cited as a barrier to planned group collaboration. Respondents nonetheless expressed strong interest in using it more for exactly that purpose.

Observation showed the same gap from the other side. Study carrels were the most frequently occupied seats, the central lounge was physically comfortable but underutilized because it offered little for academic work, and the designated meeting room often sat vacant. I recorded eleven people across my three sessions, and every one of them was working alone.

Of the survey snippets we coded by furniture type, chairs account for the most (28), then desks and study nooks (21), and tables (17), with modular or adaptable pieces (7), sofas and soft seating (6), and booths (3) trailing. Respondents asked in particular for more variety, including sofas, coffee tables, and extra desk options.

“I feel like the seating options could be a bit more varied and strategically placed. I enjoy working in a quiet space, but often my work requires collaboration, so having both in different areas would be great.”Survey respondent

a new method

In the last weeks of the study we developed a method and named it:

Thematic Archetype Analysis

It entails:

  1. 1Defining four user archetypes from documented behavior.
  2. 2Sorting each survey or interview response into one of the archetypes.
  3. 3Documenting which draws the most responses (a tie between two produces a new archetype that combines them).
  4. 4Charting how they differ.

What I recommended

I prioritized three main goals for the renovation, all anchored around re-orienting the layout around group work while maintaining the library's calm atmosphere:

  1. 1A wider range of functional seating, from workable sofas and armchairs to loungers with built-in desk features and pieces that rearrange.
  2. 2A meeting room opened up by removing its wall or replacing it with glass.
  3. 3Modular desks that can be clustered for group work.

Lower in priority, I proposed pulling the central area toward the back of the room so it feels more sheltered, trading two of its four armchairs for a sofa and side tables, and replacing the computer workstations with adjustable-height desks that give students seated and standing options. Treadmill or bicycle desks closed the list as optional additions if space, budget, and interest allow.
