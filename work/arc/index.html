The agent proposes. The reporter decides.

Entry-level intelligence reporters build a narrative from scattered sources, then draft it as many as five times over, once for every tier of clearance it has to serve. Six months in, all of it is hard: the assembling, the drafting, and the cutting that keeps each tier inside policy. The colleague who could help is just as buried. So the question waits, or it never gets asked.

The shape of the work

Writing an intelligence report is subtraction. Tier 1 goes to senior national intelligence leaders and carries the granular detail, and each level beneath it cuts further as the audience widens and the clearance drops, until what is left may reach a public readership. A report can go from several pages to one sentence.

An entry-level reporter drafts every tier by hand, and senior colleagues act as the guardrail, reviewing the tier 1 draft and the tiers that follow.

  1. 1AssemblingBuilding one coherent narrative out of a vast mixture of intelligence sources.
  2. 2DraftingProducing a separate version of the report for each level of security clearance.
  3. 3ReducingCutting and censoring information in keeping with policy.

Domain convention

The tier structure is a standard practice within the intelligence community. A report carries anywhere from one tier to five depending on material sensitivity, so the four-draft case the workspace is built around is the second-heaviest version of the problem, not the universal one. Working inside that pipeline was its own education in how much of the reporting apparatus stays invisible from outside a clearance.

What the agent proposes

The agent reads the triaged sources and proposes a structure, complete with explanations that help Jordan understand why the agent made its suggestions and how the structural component fits into the tier structure.

Designed to be overruled

Not only can every structural proposal in the workspace can be opened up, but they can also be reordered, merged, or rejected.

What a reporter can do with that reasoning is where the design sits.

  1. ReorderThe order components appear in is itself a claim about information priority.
  2. MergeCombining multiple components can cost the report clarity, so the choice stays with the reporter.
  3. RejectChallenge one and the agent does not defend it.
"Without a senior reporter to consult, the agent fills that gap."

Tiered reporting

Tiered reporting starts once Jordan has finished drafting his first tier. The agent generates the tiers beneath it, flags where a passage has to change to satisfy each tier's policy, and lets the reporter adjust what it produces before anything goes to review. The agent is always available to chat whenever Jordan needs help.

policy adherence & tone

The rule holds across every tier below the first: where a right answer exists, the agent explains it; where policy or tone leaves the call genuinely open, it lays out the range and hands the decision back to the reporter.

Final deliverable

where we would go from here

This specific slice of the intelligence reporter's workflow is built on one of the most vital aspects of their job: performing content triage.

If we were able to continue to flesh out this project, we would start there, since it remains an entirely manual process. Content triage is one of the most time-consuming and complicated processes that an intelligence reporter is responsible for, but was also frequently referenced in our interviews as a significant source of entertainment for reporters who are intrigued by the investigative process itself.
